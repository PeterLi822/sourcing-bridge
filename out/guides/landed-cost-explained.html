Landed Cost Explained: The Math Every Dropshipper Gets Wrong

Published 2026-10-14

Product cost + freight + duties = landed cost — and beginners almost always price off the wrong number. Two worked examples with real data (a $0.25 lamp and a $2.73 dog bed) show why freight, not the sticker price, decides your margin.

The Formula: Product + Freight + Duties = Landed Cost

Landed cost is the total amount it costs to get one unit of product into your customer's hands — and it is the only cost number that matters when you set a retail price. The formula is simple: product cost plus international freight plus import duties and taxes equals landed cost. (Some sellers also fold in payment processing fees and packaging; we handle fees separately in the examples below.) Beginners obsess over the product cost — the $0.25 or $2.73 number on the listing — because it is the biggest, most visible figure. But as you will see in the examples, the product cost is often the smallest part of what you actually pay. Freight from China to your market, which depends on weight, package size, and the shipping line you choose, routinely exceeds the product price. Duties and sales taxes vary by country, product category, and declared value — check your country's customs rules rather than guessing. Until you have computed all three, you do not know your cost. Everything in this guide uses real numbers from this site's product data, checked against live freight quotes.

Why a $0.25 Product Can Still Lose You Money

Look at the LED night light in our product data. The product costs $0.25. The cheapest freight to Canada is $4.74. Landed cost: $4.99. Freight is 95 percent of what you pay — you are not selling a twenty-five-cent lamp, you are selling a five-dollar delivered package. Now imagine a beginner who sees $0.25 and lists it at $4.99 retail, thinking they have doubled their money. They have made exactly zero — before payment fees, before ads, before the inevitable returns. This is the single most common math error in dropshipping: pricing off the product cost instead of the landed cost. It happens because the product price is quoted everywhere and the freight is buried three clicks deep. The fix is procedural, not intellectual: never set a price until you have written down all three numbers. Cheap products are not automatically profitable products — a $0.25 item with $4.74 freight needs the same $15 retail price as a $5 item with no freight. The product cost is trivia; the landed cost is the business.

Worked Example 1: The $4.99 LED Night Light

Let us walk the LED night light all the way to a price tag, using the real data: product cost $0.25, freight $4.74 via CJPacket JYSP Sensitive (7 to 15 days to Canada), landed cost $4.99. (Figures checked 2026-10-05; freight quotes move, so recheck before you commit.) Suppose you retail it at $14.99 — a typical price for this kind of gadget. Gross margin per unit: $14.99 minus $4.99 = $10.00. Now subtract a typical card processing fee — for example, around 2.9% plus $0.30, which on $14.99 is about $0.73 — leaving roughly $9.27. That $9.27 has to cover your advertising cost per order, returns, and finally your profit. If your ads cost you, say, $5 per order (an illustrative example — your actual ad costs depend on your channels and creative), you keep about $4.27 per sale. That is a workable beginner product: nothing spectacular, but the math survives contact with reality. Note what made it work — not the $0.25 product cost, but the discipline of pricing at 3x landed cost and accounting for the fee before celebrating.

Worked Example 2: The $16.53 Plush Dog Bed

Now the same exercise with a bulky item — the round plush dog bed. Real data: product cost $2.73, freight $13.80 via CJPacket Eub (4 to 8 days to Canada), landed cost $16.53. The product is eleven times more expensive than the LED light, but the landed cost is only about three times higher — because freight dominates both. At a $39.99 retail price: $39.99 minus $16.53 = $23.46 gross; minus the card fee of about $1.46 (2.9% plus $0.30 on $39.99) = roughly $22.00 before ads. With the same illustrative $5 ad cost per order, you keep about $17 per sale — four times the LED light's profit, on a product that costs only $2.73. Two lessons here. First, absolute profit per sale matters more than margin percentage: a 2.4x markup on the dog bed beats 3x on the lamp in dollars. Second, bulky freight is not a dealbreaker if the category supports the retail price — pet owners happily pay $39.99 for their dog's comfort, which is why the category absorbs a $13.80 freight bill that would kill a novelty gadget.

Five Margin Mistakes Beginners Make

Five mistakes that quietly eat beginners' margins. One: pricing off the product cost — covered above, but it deserves repetition because everyone does it once. Two: forgetting return freight. A returned item often costs you the outbound freight twice (there and back) plus a refund; on a $13.80-freight dog bed, one return wipes out the profit of nearly one sale. Price with a returns buffer, or tighten your product page until returns are rare. Three: always picking the slowest freight line to save a dollar. If the $4.74 line takes 15 days and a $5.50 line takes 7, the faster line's conversion lift usually dwarfs the $0.76 — test it rather than assuming. Four: ignoring dimensional weight. Carriers charge by whichever is greater: actual weight or size-derived weight. A 468-gram dog bed in a big box can be billed like a much heavier item — always check the package dims, not just the grams. Five: treating quotes as permanent. Freight is a market, not a menu; recheck before every reorder.
